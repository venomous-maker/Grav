# Decimal

`import "std/types"` (included in `import "std"`) · namespace `std.types`

`Decimal` is an exact decimal number, for money and for databases' DECIMAL and
NUMERIC columns, where a `float`'s binary rounding will not do: `0.1 + 0.2` is
`0.30000000000000004` as floats and exactly `0.3` as Decimals.

```grav
import "std"

fn main() {
    let price = Decimal.parse("19.99")
    let total = price * Decimal.of(3)
    print(total)
    print(total + Decimal.parse("0.01"))
    print(Decimal.fromFloat(0.1) + Decimal.fromFloat(0.2))
    print(Decimal.of(1).div(Decimal.of(3), 4))
    print(Decimal.parse("2.345").round(2))
    print(Decimal.parse("19.90") == Decimal.parse("19.9"))
}
```

Output:

```text
59.97
59.98
0.3
0.3333
2.35
true
```

- A Decimal holds up to **38 significant digits**, with up to 38 of them after
  the point: its **scale**. It keeps the scale it was written with, so
  `19.90` prints as `19.90`, though `==` and the other comparisons find it
  equal to `19.9`.
- `+` and `-` give the larger of the two scales, `*` their sum (`1.10 * 2.5`
  is `2.750`). Division needs the scale you want: `a.div(b, 2)`. `div` and
  `round(n)` round **half away from zero** (`2.345` to `2.35`, `-2.345` to
  `-2.35`).
- Making one: `Decimal.parse("19.99")` (also `-0.5`, `+3`, `.25`, `1.5e3`),
  `Decimal.tryParse` (null instead of a ValueError), `Decimal.of(42)`,
  `Decimal.of(1999, 2)` (19.99), and `Decimal.fromFloat(0.1)`, the shortest
  decimal that reads back as that float.
- Getting a number back: `toString()` writes every digit of its scale,
  `toFloat()` gives the nearest float, `toLong()` the whole part toward zero.
- A result past 38 digits, and division by zero, are `ArithmeticError`s.
